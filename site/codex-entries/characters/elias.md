Seventy-one years old. Former data architect. He helped design The Accord in its early years — the system that now runs the city's resource allocation.

He left in 2038. He has not spoken publicly about why.

He was married once, for a long time. She left before the work took everything from him — she saw where it was going, he says, before he let himself see it. He does not blame her, and has had a long time not to.

There is also a daughter. She believes he has been dead for six years — a notice, a closure in his file, no body she ever saw. He has let her believe it. He tells himself his silence is the one protection he can still offer her: that if she knew he was alive, the systems that still watch, faintly, for exactly that kind of reappearance would turn toward her too. He has never tested whether that is true, or whether the silence protects him instead. After nine years he decides on the smallest gesture that asks nothing back — a seed and an unsigned line, sent by a route that cannot be traced to him. It isn't enough. He knows it isn't enough. He does it anyway.

His hands are always dirty. He grows things in a disused rail yard on the edge of the district. He calls it a garden. The city's systems don't register it.

He moves carefully, as if he has learned to take up less space than he used to.

He is ill. He noticed the first signs months before he let himself act on them — an ache he named *not yet*, the same way he once named the thing he stayed silent about for nine years. By the time he finally goes to a clinic, the diagnosis is a mass, operable in principle, on terms less forgiving than they would have been had he walked in sooner. He does not fall apart when he reads it. The falling apart, with him, tends to arrive later, sideways, disguised as something smaller.
