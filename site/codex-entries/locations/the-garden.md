A disused rail yard at the edge of the district. No official designation. The Accord's mapping systems show it as unclassified infrastructure.

Elias Voss tends it. He grows root vegetables, mostly. Some herbs. A few things he cannot name anymore because he has forgotten the names and does not want to look them up.

The soil is called loam. It holds water without drowning roots. It gives without demanding that you account for what it gives.

Nothing about this place appears in any system. That is not an accident.

At the end of June 2047, that changes. For the first time in seven years, The Accord addresses the place directly: a remediation notice, sealed against weather, fixed to the gap in the fence and addressed not to a person but to a parcel — *9-R-114*. Clearance is scheduled to commence no earlier than thirty days from the notice, under the Northern Expansion Priority Corridor framework. No relocation assistance is offered for unregistered land use. The garden the system never registered now has a letter of its own.

The crew comes on a Tuesday morning in late July, earlier than the notice implied. Their order says *decommissioned rail infrastructure*; it says nothing about seven years of soil and structure and care, and the foreman — younger than expected, not unkind — has no word in his paperwork for what he finds. Elias asks only that the record note the place had been a garden since 2040, for whatever that is worth to whoever reads it after. The first day's work is the fencing: rolls of orange mesh unloaded to surround everything, marking the boundary of the place for its own ending.

The day before the clearance proper, the same foreman offers Elias one last walk of it — required by no protocol, offered anyway. The beds are already empty: the beet bed turned to dark ordinary soil, the brassica rows cut to stubble, the cold frame clean and holding nothing, the south bed reduced to dry stakes and jute where the sweet peas once climbed. What was worth keeping has gone to Daniel's strip of soil or into two tins. He walks it at his ordinary unhurried pace, does not look back a second time, signs what needs signing, and steps through the gate for the last time.
